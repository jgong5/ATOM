A prefill step's cost is not a function of its token count

The previous sweep varied --num-prompts at a fixed prompt length, so
token count and sequence count moved together: 1094 tokens was one
sequence and 13530 was twelve. "Overhead rises with tokens" was
reported from it and is not supported by it. Decoupled, by holding
total tokens roughly fixed and redistributing them:

  tokens  seqs      step   ms/1k tok
    3952     8   55.255ms      13.98
    4376     4   63.232ms      14.45
    4588     2   52.288ms      11.40
    4694     1   65.027ms      13.85
    8752     8  104.479ms      11.94
    9176     4  100.278ms      10.93
    9388     2  121.868ms      12.98
   10094     1  174.534ms      17.29

At ~9-10k tokens one long sequence costs 174.5ms where four shorter
ones totalling 9176 cost 100.3 -- 74% more time for 10% more tokens.
At ~4.5k the same comparison is 18% and the group has no clean
ordering.

That is attention being quadratic within a sequence: 10094 squared
against 4 x 2294 squared is 4.8x the attention work, and attention's
share of a prefill grows with size, which is why the effect is large
at 10k and lost in the noise at 4.5k.

So a prefill shape is not a scalar but the multiset of sequence
lengths, and two steps with identical token counts can differ by 74%.
Measuring the shapes that occur is not merely expensive but
ill-defined: there is no enumerable set, the way decode has rungs.

This does not undermine the op graph, which already records
cu_seqlens_q per attention call. The quadratic quantity is visible at
operator level and invisible at step level, which argues for
empirical/fitted per operator -- attention against its own sequence
lengths, gemms against total tokens -- rather than for any amount of
step-level measurement. It is the first place the op-graph approach is
clearly necessary rather than merely tidier.
