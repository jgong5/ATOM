Probe prefill shapes: it does not interpolate, and the dispatch term
has the wrong sign

Decode was closed by measuring an enumerable set. Prefill token counts
are not enumerable, so the hope was that a step -- hundreds of kernels
-- would be smooth in tokens even where a single gemm is not, letting
a handful of measured sizes cover the range. Six sizes traced, priced
at 99.2%, and measured:

  tokens   priced  measured  ratio  overhead/op
    1094  15.766ms  31.746ms   0.50     50.1us
    2188  28.460ms  31.985ms   0.89     11.0us
    4376  60.685ms  63.291ms   0.96      8.2us
    6564  76.218ms  79.135ms   0.96      9.1us
    8752 100.459ms 104.421ms   0.96     12.4us
   13530 127.520ms 132.948ms   0.96     17.0us

The hypothesis fails: interpolating a measured step between measured
neighbours gives median 12.2% and worst 32.1%, against 2.1% for the
decode ladder which measures exactly.

Two things fall out that are worth more. The dispatch term is wrong
for prefill on this model -- measured overhead per operator is 8 to
50us where dispatch_seconds charges up to 130, giving +35% at 1094
tokens and +59% at 2188 against +4.2% at 13530 -- and the measured
overhead rises with token count above 4k where "dispatch the kernels
hide" predicts it should fall. The model has the sign wrong in the
range that matters. Meanwhile priced kernels alone are 0.96 of a
prefill step above 4k, so no overhead term at all beats the one we
have for every size but the smallest.

And prefill depends on sequence count as well as tokens: 1256 tokens
in four sequences measured 42.6ms where 1094 in one measured 31.7ms,
because attention pays per sequence. Its shape is at least
two-dimensional and every measurement here varies one dimension.

So prefill needs a fitted form -- empirical/fitted at operator level,
the empty cell of the grid -- or derivation. It is the one part of the
cost model where measuring the shapes that occur is not viable.
